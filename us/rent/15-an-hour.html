30% rule vs 40× rent rule: how much rent $15 an hour affords

$15 an hour for a full-time 40-hour week is $31,200 a year (2,080 hours), or $2,600 a month before taxes. Applying the most common rent rules of thumb to that gross figure gives these monthly limits:

25% of gross$650comfortable
30% rule / 40× rule$780standard ceiling
3× rent rule$867landlord minimum
30% of take-home$681no-tax state

Landlords typically screen with one of two income tests. The 40× rule (annual pay ≥ 40 × monthly rent) lets you rent up to $780 — exactly what the 30% rule gives. The 3× rule (monthly pay ≥ 3 × rent) is slightly more lenient at $867. Neither one looks at taxes, debt or savings, which is why they are ceilings rather than targets.

At this income the rules of thumb and real-world rents collide. A $780 budget is 52% of the U.S. median gross rent, so in most large metros the realistic options are a room in a shared apartment, a studio in a lower-cost neighborhood, or income-restricted (LIHTC) housing, which is typically built for households at or below 60% of area median income.

$15/hour after taxes: take-home pay and rent in three states

Rent comes out of take-home pay, not gross pay, so it helps to see what's actually left. For tax year 2026, a single filer earning $31,200 with the $16,100 standard deduction owes about $1,564 in federal income tax and $2,387 in Social Security and Medicare (FICA). State income tax is where things diverge: Texas, Florida, Washington and six other states don't tax wages, while California and New York do.

50/30/20 budget on $15 an hour: where rent fits

The 50/30/20 rule splits take-home pay into needs (50%), wants (30%) and savings or extra debt payments (20%). Using the no-tax-state take-home of $2,271 a month, that's $1,135 for needs, $681 for wants and $454 for savings. Rent and utilities have to fit inside the needs bucket together with groceries, transportation, insurance and minimum debt payments.

Tips for renting on $15 an hour

  • Look for a room in a shared house or apartment first; it is often 40%–60% of a one-bedroom's rent.
  • Ask your local housing authority or 211 about income-restricted (LIHTC) apartments and Housing Choice Voucher waiting lists.
  • If a landlord wants 40× rent, ask whether they accept a guarantor (usually 80× rent) or an institutional guarantor service.
  • Budget for move-in costs: first month, a security deposit and application fees can add up to two or three months of rent.
  • Check whether you qualify for the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC); for lower earners it can boost the yearly cash you have for housing.

FAQ: rent on $15 an hour

How much rent can I afford making $15 an hour?

About $780 a month if you work 40 hours a week. $15 an hour is $31,200 a year, and the 30% rule (the same as the 40× rent rule) caps rent at $31,200 ÷ 40 = $780. Budgeting from take-home pay gives a more comfortable $681.

$15 an hour is how much a month after taxes?

About $2,271 a month in a state with no income tax, $2,250 in California and $2,180 in New York (state tax only), for a single filer working 2,080 hours with the 2026 standard deduction. Gross monthly pay is $2,600.

Is $15 an hour enough to rent an apartment by myself?

In most big cities, not without a roommate: the cheapest one-bedroom benchmark on our list, Dallas at $1,536, is above your $780 budget. Lower-cost cities, a studio or a shared apartment are the realistic options.

What hourly wage do I need for a $2,700 apartment?

Under the 40× rule you need about $108,000 a year, which is $51.92 an hour full-time. That's roughly a typical one-bedroom in New York City.

Do landlords count overtime and tips?

Usually only if you can document them consistently — recent pay stubs, a W-2 or tax return. If your hours vary, plan your rent on the hours you are sure to work, and consider a guarantor (often 80× the rent) if you fall short of 40×.
