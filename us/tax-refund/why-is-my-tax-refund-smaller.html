Refund troubleshooting

Why is my tax refund smaller this year?

A refund is just the gap between what was withheld and what you owe. Here is what usually shrinks that gap — with real numbers.

Short answer

Your refund shrinks when less tax was withheld or your tax (after credits) went up. The most common culprits: a child turning 17 (the $2,200 Child Tax Credit becomes a $500 credit), changes to your W-4, losing head-of-household status, a raise that phases out the EITC, side income with no withholding, and IRS offsets for debts. A smaller refund isn’t necessarily bad — it can mean bigger paychecks during the year.

The refund formula in one line

Refund = federal income tax withheld − tax after non-refundable credits + refundable credits. Every reason below moves one of those three pieces. Social Security and Medicare are separate and never come back as a refund.

To make it concrete, picture a head of household earning $55,000 with one child and a W-4 that only has the filing status checked. Her estimated 2026 refund is $2,200. Here is how much each change would cut it:

1. A child turned 17

The Child Tax Credit only covers children under 17 at the end of the year. The year your oldest turns 17, the $2,200 credit becomes the $500 credit for other dependents — and the $500 credit isn’t refundable. In our example that alone costs $1,700. Nothing went wrong; the rules simply changed for that child. See the Child Tax Credit 2026 guide.

2. You (or payroll) changed your W-4

If you entered your kids in Step 3 or the new deductions in Step 4(b), less tax came out of each check. The total benefit is the same — you just got it in 26 smaller pieces instead of one spring lump. Entering one child in Step 3 raises a biweekly paycheck by about $85 and lowers the refund to $0.

See how to adjust your W-4 if you’d rather have the refund back.

3–5. Filing status, raises and side income

Lost head-of-household status. If your child moved out or you married, you may be filing single or jointly instead. Filing single with the same $55,000 and the same withholding costs about $1,072 here, because the standard deduction drops from $24,150 to $16,100 and the 12% bracket starts sooner.

A raise that phases out credits. Withholding handles raises fine — but credits don’t. Going from $45,000 to $52,000 as a head of household with one child reduces the EITC, so the refund drops by about $1,054 even though take-home pay rose.

Side income. $4,000 of 1099 gig profit has no withholding. Income tax at 12% plus self-employment tax adds roughly $1,011 to the bill, which comes straight out of the refund.

6–8. One-off boosts, offsets and IRS corrections

  • Last year’s refund had one-time help. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act was signed in July 2025 but applied to the whole 2025 year, and 2025 withholding tables weren’t updated mid-year. Many people’s 2025 refunds (paid in early 2026) included the catch-up. The 2026 tables already reflect the new standard deduction and brackets, so that part doesn’t repeat — the IRS average refund jumped to $3,273 in 2026, an unusually high bar to compare against.
  • Treasury offsets. Past-due child support, state tax debts, unemployment overpayments and defaulted federal student loans can be deducted before the refund is sent. You’ll get a notice from the Bureau of the Fiscal Service.
  • IRS math corrections. If the IRS disallows a dependent or fixes a credit, it adjusts the refund and mails an explanation.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my tax refund so low this year?

Usually because less tax was withheld (a W-4 change, a new job) or your tax after credits rose (a child turned 17, you lost head-of-household status, a raise phased out the EITC, or you had untaxed side income). IRS offsets for debts can also reduce it.

Does a smaller refund mean I paid more tax?

Not necessarily. If your paychecks were bigger because less was withheld, you received the same money earlier. Compare your total tax (Form 1040 “total tax” line), not just the refund.

My child turned 17 — how much less will I get?

The $2,200 Child Tax Credit is replaced by the $500 credit for other dependents, which isn’t refundable. For most families that’s about $1,700 less.

Why did my refund go down when I got a raise?

Withholding keeps pace with raises, but the Earned Income Tax Credit phases out as income rises. Lower-income families can lose several hundred dollars of EITC for a few thousand dollars of raise.

Why was my refund less than what Where’s My Refund said?

If the deposit is lower than the approved amount, it was probably reduced by the Treasury Offset Program for a debt. You’ll receive a notice explaining the offset.
