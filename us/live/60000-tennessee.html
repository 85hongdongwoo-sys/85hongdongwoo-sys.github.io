$60k a year is how much a month in Tennessee? Compared with what people earn

$60,000 works out to $5,000 a month before tax and $4,199 after. To judge whether that's "good", compare it with the people around you. The U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey puts median earnings for a Tennessee worker at $43,255 (all workers with earnings, 2024 ACS 1-year) and median household income at $75,875 (2025 ACS 1-year, released October 2026). Your salary is 39% above the typical individual and 21% below the typical household, which often has two earners.

Median earnings include part-time workers, so full-time salaries naturally sit above it. National medians: earnings $46,949, household income $84,780.

How much rent can you afford on $60k in Tennessee?

The 30% rule says housing (rent plus utilities) should stay at or below 30% of gross income. On $60,000 that is $1,500 a month. A stricter version uses take-home pay: 30% of $4,199 is $1,260.

The median gross rent in Tennessee is $1,284 a month (rent plus utilities, ACS 2024). That is only 26% of your gross monthly pay — inside the classic 30% guideline — so a typical apartment is within reach without roommates.

Can you live on $60k in Nashville?

Tennessee does not tax wages, but its combined state and local sales tax is among the highest in the country, which matters for everyday spending. Nashville has become much pricier than Memphis, Knoxville or Chattanooga.

MIT's Living Wage Calculator (data updated February 2026) puts the living wage for a single adult with no children in Tennessee at $21.61 an hour, or $44,959 a year before tax. $60,000 equals $28.85 an hour for a 40-hour week — $7.24 an hour above that line.

In Nashville, $60k is workable for one person, especially outside the most in-demand neighborhoods, but it is not a salary that makes big-city living effortless. Nashville's rents rose quickly with its job growth; Memphis, Knoxville and Chattanooga generally offer lower housing costs for the same salary.

How much can you save on $60,000 in Tennessee?

Setting aside the 20% share — about $840 a month or $10,078 a year — would build a six-month emergency fund ($12,598, based on the 50% needs bucket) in about 15 months. Kept up for ten years at an assumed 5% annual return, those deposits would grow to roughly $130,411 — an illustration, not a forecast. Contributing to a 401(k) also lowers taxable income: each $1,000 put in pre-tax saves about $120 in federal income tax at this salary.
