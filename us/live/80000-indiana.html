$80k a year is how much a month in Indiana? Compared with what people earn

$80,000 works out to $6,667 a month before tax and $5,232 after. To judge whether that's "good", compare it with the people around you. The U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey puts median earnings for a Indiana worker at $44,095 (all workers with earnings, 2024 ACS 1-year) and median household income at $74,516 (2025 ACS 1-year, released October 2026). Your salary is 81% above the typical individual and 7% above the typical household, which often has two earners.

Median earnings include part-time workers, so full-time salaries naturally sit above it. National medians: earnings $46,949, household income $84,780.

How much rent can you afford on $80k in Indiana?

The 30% rule says housing (rent plus utilities) should stay at or below 30% of gross income. On $80,000 that is $2,000 a month. A stricter version uses take-home pay: 30% of $5,232 is $1,569.

The median gross rent in Indiana is $1,104 a month (rent plus utilities, ACS 2024). That is only 17% of your gross monthly pay — inside the classic 30% guideline — so a typical apartment is within reach without roommates.

Can you live on $80k in Indianapolis?

Indiana has a low flat state income tax, but every county adds its own county income tax (roughly 0.5%–3%, not included here). Rents are well below the national median.

MIT's Living Wage Calculator (data updated February 2026) puts the living wage for a single adult with no children in Indiana at $21.79 an hour, or $45,326 a year before tax. $80,000 equals $38.46 an hour for a 40-hour week — $16.67 an hour above that line.

In Indianapolis, $80k is workable for one person, especially outside the most in-demand neighborhoods, but it is not a salary that makes big-city living effortless. Indianapolis is one of the more affordable large metros in the Midwest; suburbs such as Carmel and Fishers cost more, while many older city neighborhoods remain inexpensive.

How much can you save on $80,000 in Indiana?

Setting aside the 20% share — about $1,046 a month or $12,556 a year — would build a six-month emergency fund ($15,695, based on the 50% needs bucket) in about 15 months. Kept up for ten years at an assumed 5% annual return, those deposits would grow to roughly $162,476 — an illustration, not a forecast. Contributing to a 401(k) also lowers taxable income: each $1,000 put in pre-tax saves about $250 in federal and state income tax at this salary.
