Home loan tax benefit: old vs new regime

In the old regime you can deduct up to ₹2 lakh of home-loan interest and up to ₹1.5 lakh of principal (80C) a year; the new regime gives neither for a self-occupied home. Yet for most salaried borrowers the new regime still costs less tax in FY 2026-27 — here are the numbers.

From 1 April 2026 the Income-tax Act 2025 replaced the 1961 Act and section numbers changed, but these limits were carried over; most people still refer to the interest deduction as “Section 24(b)”. The new regime is the default — you must opt for the old regime when filing your return (salaried taxpayers can switch each year).

Old vs new regime with a ₹50 lakh home loan

Say you have a ₹50 lakh loan at 8% for 20 years and live in the home. First-year interest is ₹3,96,181 (so the full ₹2 lakh is claimable) and principal is ₹1,05,683. The result surprises many borrowers: with a typical salary and only the usual deductions (₹2 lakh interest, ₹1.5 lakh 80C, ₹25,000 80D — ₹3.75 lakh in all), the new regime is cheaper at every income in the table. Up to about 13–14 LPA the ₹12 lakh rebate brings new-regime tax to nil; above that, the new regime’s wider slabs (30% only above ₹24 lakh) outweigh the deductions. For the old regime to win you would need the extra deductions shown in the last column — for example HRA because you live in a rented home in another city, NPS under 80CCD(1B) (₹50,000), or interest on a let-out second property. The figures are for this example only — run your own numbers.

Joint home loans double the benefit

If two people are both co-borrowers and co-owners, each can claim up to ₹2 lakh of interest and up to ₹1.5 lakh of principal (within their own 80C) under the old regime — in the proportion of their share of the EMI. On a ₹50 lakh loan with ₹3,96,181 of first-year interest, a couple sharing 50:50 can claim ₹1,98,090 each. Being only a co-borrower without ownership does not qualify.

Conditions that trip people up

  • Construction must finish within 5 years from the end of the financial year in which the loan was taken, otherwise the self-occupied interest cap falls to ₹30,000.
  • The 80C principal deduction is reversed if you sell the house within 5 years of the end of the year you took possession.
  • Interest on a loan from friends or family is allowed (with a certificate), but principal repaid to them does not qualify for 80C.
  • The special first-time-buyer deductions (80EE, 80EEA) are closed for loans sanctioned after 31 March 2022.
  • Keep the bank’s interest certificate each year; employers need it to reflect the deduction in TDS if you choose the old regime.

Two self-occupied homes

You can treat up to two houses as self-occupied, but the combined interest deduction for both remains ₹2 lakh. A third house is treated as let out (deemed rent is taxable).

FAQ: home loan tax benefits

Can I claim home loan interest in the new tax regime?

Not for a self-occupied home. For a let-out property, interest is deductible against rental income, but any loss cannot be set off against salary or carried forward.

What is the maximum home loan interest deduction?

₹2 lakh a year for a self-occupied home under the old regime (Section 24(b)); for a let-out property the interest is fully deductible from rent but the loss set-off against other income is limited to ₹2 lakh.

Is home loan principal deductible?

Yes, in the old regime, under 80C within the overall ₹1.5 lakh limit shared with EPF, PPF, ELSS and life insurance.

Can both spouses claim home loan tax benefits?

Yes, if both are co-owners and co-borrowers. Each can claim up to ₹2 lakh of interest and ₹1.5 lakh under 80C in the old regime, in proportion to their share of the repayment.

Which regime is better with a ₹50 lakh home loan?

In our example (typical salary structure, ₹2 lakh interest, ₹1.5 lakh 80C, ₹25,000 80D, no HRA) the new regime is cheaper at every income from 12 to 50 LPA. The old regime wins only with substantial extra deductions such as HRA or NPS, so compare both every year.
