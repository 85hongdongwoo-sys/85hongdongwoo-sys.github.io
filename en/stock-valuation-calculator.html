    How the residual income model (S-RIM) thinks

    Residual income valuation says a share is worth its book value plus the present value of any earnings above what shareholders require. S-RIM is a simplified version popularized in Korea by accountant Sa Kyung-in. Shareholders want the capital they have left in the company (BPS) to earn at least the required return r. If ROE is above r, the company creates excess earnings = BPS × (ROE − r) every year; their present value is added to BPS. If they last forever you divide by r; if they fade 10% a year you use ω = 0.9. Conversely, when ROE is below r, value falls below book, so a P/B under 1 is not automatically cheap.

    The "persistent" S-RIM value equals BPS × ROE ÷ r — the same as theoretical P/B (ROE ÷ r) × BPS.

    Choosing the required return r

    The Korean S-RIM convention uses the BBB- corporate bond yield as a practical "what investors could earn instead" rate. The closest free US series is the ICE BofA BBB US Corporate Index effective yield (FRED: BAMLC0A4CBBBEY), 6.01% on Oct 29, 2026 — the default here. A more conventional US cost-of-equity approach is the 10-year Treasury yield plus an equity risk premium: 4.98% (Oct 29, 2026) + 4–5% ≈ 9–10%. A higher r gives lower S-RIM values and a lower theoretical P/B, so try both.

    Frequently asked questions

    What do P/E, P/B and ROE mean?

    P/E (price-to-earnings) is share price ÷ earnings per share (EPS): how many years of current earnings you pay for. P/B (price-to-book) is price ÷ book value per share (BPS): how many times its accounting net worth the stock trades at. ROE (return on equity) is net income ÷ shareholders' equity: how many cents a company earns per dollar of shareholder capital each year. They are linked by P/B = P/E × ROE.

    What is S-RIM?

    S-RIM is a simplified residual income model popularized in Korea by accountant Sa Kyung-in (2017). Fair value = BPS + BPS × (ROE − r) ÷ r, where r is the required return. It adds the value of "excess earnings" above what shareholders require to book value. If excess earnings are expected to fade by 10% or 20% a year, a persistence factor ω = 0.9 or 0.8 is used: BPS + excess earnings × ω ÷ (1 + r − ω). The Korean method uses the BBB- corporate bond yield as r; this US version defaults to the ICE BofA BBB US Corporate Index effective yield.

    Is a low P/E stock always cheap?

    No. If earnings are temporarily inflated, the industry is shrinking, or ROE is below the required return so the company destroys shareholder value, a stock can stay at a low P/E and P/B for years (a value trap). Read a low P/E together with ROE, earnings growth, dividends and debt. When ROE is below the required return, a P/B under 1 can be fair.
