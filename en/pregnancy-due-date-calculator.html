    How the due date is calculated

    Pregnancy is counted from the first day of your last menstrual period (LMP), not from conception. Ovulation usually happens about two weeks later, so at conception you are already “2 weeks pregnant”. An average pregnancy lasts 280 days (40 weeks) from the LMP.

    Last periodLMP + 280 days + (cycle − 28)
    ConceptionConception + 266 days
    IVF, day-3 embryoTransfer + 263 days
    IVF, day-5 blastocystTransfer + 261 days
    UltrasoundScan date − measured age + 280 days
    Trimesters1st to 13w6d · 2nd 14w0d–27w6d · 3rd 28w0d+

    ACOG advises that the most accurate dating comes from an IVF transfer date or a first-trimester ultrasound measurement (crown–rump length). If an early scan differs from the LMP date by more than a set margin — for example more than 5 days before 9 weeks or more than 7 days from 9 to 13 weeks — the ultrasound date is used.     Frequently asked questions

    How is a due date calculated?
    The standard rule (Naegele's rule) adds 280 days, or 40 weeks, to the first day of your last menstrual period, assuming a 28-day cycle. If your cycle is longer or shorter, the date shifts by the difference. From a known conception date add 266 days; after IVF add 263 days to a day-3 embryo transfer or 261 days to a day-5 blastocyst transfer.
    Why am I already 2 weeks pregnant at conception?
    Pregnancy is dated from the first day of the last period, not from conception. Ovulation and fertilization usually happen about two weeks later, so at the moment of conception the gestational age is already about 2 weeks.
    My doctor gave me a different due date. Which is right?
    Use your clinician's date. ACOG recommends that an early ultrasound be used to confirm or change the due date when it differs from the period-based date by more than a set margin, and that the date not be changed later without good reason.
