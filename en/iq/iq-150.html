What Does an IQ of 150 Mean?

Percentile, rarity, classification and what research really says about a score of 150 — computed on the standard scale (mean 100, SD 15).

An IQ of 150 is at the 99.957th percentile: it is higher than about 99.957% of people of the same age, and about 1 in 2,330 people scores 150 or higher. On the WAIS-IV it is classed as Very Superior. Like every IQ score it is an estimate with a margin of error of several points, and it predicts outcomes only on average.

IQ 150 percentile and how it is calculated

IQ tests are scaled so that scores follow an approximately normal distribution with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation (SD) of 15. A score of 150 is 3.33 standard deviations above the mean (z = +3.333). Plugging that into the normal cumulative distribution function gives a percentile of 99.957. Put simply: in a representative group of 1,000 people of your age, about 0 would score 150 or higher.

Some reports use a slightly different convention — for example the percentage of people scoring strictly below 150, or a value read from the test’s own norm tables rather than the ideal curve — so you may see a percentile a fraction of a point different. Is 150 a good IQ score?

Yes — 150 is extremely rare (about 1 in 2,300) and near the ceiling of most tests. See also what counts as a good IQ score.

How rare is a 150 IQ?

On the normal model, about 1 in 2,330 people scores 150 or higher. That is about 429 per million people, or roughly 21,500 people in a country of 50 million. Rarity grows quickly in the tails: each extra 5 points above 100 multiplies the rarity, which is why the step from 150 to 155 (1 in 2,330 → 1 in 8,140) is much bigger than the step from 145 to 150.

What an IQ of 150 means in practice

An IQ of 150 is 3.33 standard deviations above the mean, at about the 99.96th percentile: roughly one person in 2,330. On the WAIS-IV, whose full-scale range tops out at 160, 150 is already near the ceiling.

At this level a score says as much about the test as about the person. Ceiling effects, the precise norm table used, and whether the test is timed all change the result. Claims of “150+” from online tests are almost never meaningful, because those tests rarely have norm samples large enough to identify one person in 2,000 accurately.

Many reported “genius IQs” for famous historical figures are retrospective estimates or ratio-IQ figures from childhood tests (mental age divided by chronological age), which are not comparable to modern deviation IQ scores.

150 IQ jobs, education and life outcomes: what research says

Large meta-analyses agree that IQ predicts educational and occupational outcomes on average, with plenty of individual exceptions. Strenze (2007), pooling longitudinal studies, found corrected correlations of about .56 between intelligence and education, .43 with occupational level and .20 with income. For job performance, Schmidt and Hunter (1998) estimated a validity of about .51 for general mental ability; a 2022 re-analysis by Sackett and colleagues revised it to about .31, still useful but leaving most differences in performance to other factors such as experience, conscientiousness and job knowledge.

Measurement error: how precise is a score of 150?

A full-length clinical test such as the WAIS-IV or WAIS-5 has very high reliability (internal consistency around .97–.98 for the full-scale IQ), which still leaves a standard error of measurement (SEM) of about 2–3 points; Pearson’s WAIS-5 sample report lists an FSIQ SEM of 3.00. A 95% confidence interval is roughly ±1.96 SEM, so a measured 150 is best read as about 144 to 156. Shorter tests have larger errors (SEM 4–5 or more), widening the band to roughly 141–159.

Extreme scores also tend to move toward the mean on re-testing (regression to the mean). With a reliability of .96, the best estimate of the “true” score behind an observed 150 is about 148.0, which is why many reports centre the confidence interval slightly below the obtained score.

What an IQ of 150 does not tell you

  • It is a sample of performance on one day; sleep, stress, language and familiarity with tests all affect it.
  • Index scores (verbal, spatial, reasoning, memory, speed) can differ widely behind the same full-scale number.
  • It does not fix the future: correlations of .2 to .6 leave room for very different outcomes among people with the same score.
  • It cannot be compared across very different tests or eras without adjusting for norms.
  • It does not measure creativity, practical wisdom, emotional skills, motivation or character.

The Flynn effect and old norms

IQ is always relative to a norm group, and norm groups age. Because average raw performance rose by roughly 3 points per decade for much of the 20th century (Flynn effect; Trahan et al., 2014), an outdated test makes everyone look better. Scored on norms 15 years old, a 150 might correspond to about 146 on fresh norms. Gains have slowed and even reversed in some countries such as Norway (Bratsberg & Rogeberg, 2018), so the size of the effect is not fixed.

Online IQ tests and a score of 150

If your 150 came from a website or app, it may be off by 10 points or more. Unproctored tests, unknown norms, practice effects and flattering scoring are all common. How to get a proper IQ assessment

A valid IQ score comes from an individually administered, standardized test (for example the WAIS-5/WAIS-IV for adults, WISC-V for children, or Stanford-Binet 5) given by a licensed or registered psychologist. Expect 60–120 minutes of testing, a written report with index scores and confidence intervals, and a fee that varies by country and provider. Schools, university disability services, and clinical or neuropsychology services can refer you. Ask which test and which norms were used, and for the confidence interval, not just the single number.
