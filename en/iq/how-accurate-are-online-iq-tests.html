How Accurate Are Online IQ Tests?

Why online scores are often off by 10 points or more, how to spot a better test, and where to get a real assessment.

Most online IQ tests are not accurate enough to give you a real IQ score. They usually lack a representative norm sample, run unsupervised, can be retaken, and some inflate results to sell certificates. A few are reasonable as rough indicators, but only an individually administered, standardized test (such as the WAIS or WISC) given by a psychologist yields a valid IQ — with a margin of error of about ±5–6 points.

Why online IQ tests are usually inaccurate

  1. No real norm sample. An IQ score is a rank against a representative group of people your age. Many websites never test such a group; others norm on their own self-selected visitors, who differ from the general population.
  2. Too few, too similar items. Clinical tests combine 10 or more subtests (verbal, visual-spatial, reasoning, memory, speed). Many online tests use 20–40 matrix puzzles only, which measures a narrow slice of ability.
  3. No supervision. You can pause, look things up, use a calculator, ask a friend or retake the test. Each retake adds practice effects.
  4. Flattering scoring. Some commercial tests report high numbers to encourage sharing and to sell detailed reports or certificates.
  5. Ceiling and floor problems. With few very hard items, a test cannot meaningfully distinguish 140 from 160; claims of very high scores are especially unreliable.
  6. No confidence interval. A serious report gives a range, not just a single number.

Are any online IQ tests accurate?

Some are better than others. Research-grade item banks such as the International Cognitive Ability Resource (ICAR; Condon & Revelle, 2014) are public-domain, have published psychometric properties and are used in large studies — but they are designed for research on groups, not for diagnosing individuals. Some commercial tests report validation against established tests; check whether they publish their norm sample, reliability and correlation with a clinical test, and whether these were peer-reviewed.

Mensa and other high-IQ societies

Mensa requires a score in the top 2% on an approved, supervised test — about 130 on an SD-15 scale. Online results are not accepted for admission. National Mensa groups run supervised entrance tests or accept prior qualifying scores from recognised tests.

How to get an accurate IQ score

  • Ask a licensed or registered (clinical, educational or neuro-) psychologist for a full assessment with a standardized test such as the WAIS-5/WAIS-IV, WISC-V or Stanford-Binet 5.
  • Expect 1–2 hours of testing and a written report with index scores and a 95% confidence interval.
  • Schools, universities (disability or learning-support services) and health services can often refer or provide assessment when there is a clinical or educational reason.
  • Sleep well and take the test as you are — preparation or practice on similar items can inflate scores and reduce their usefulness.

Frequently asked questions

Are online IQ tests accurate?

Usually not. Most lack a representative norm sample and supervision. They can be fun or rough indicators, but they do not give a valid IQ score.

Why did I score higher on an online IQ test?

Common reasons include easy items, flattering scoring, no time limit, retakes and norms based on self-selected visitors.

Is there a free accurate IQ test online?

Research tools such as ICAR have documented properties, but they are designed for group research. A valid individual IQ requires a standardized test given by a psychologist.

Does Mensa accept online IQ tests?

No. Mensa requires a top-2% score on an approved, supervised test.

How accurate is a professional IQ test?

Very reliable: full-scale reliability around .97–.98 and an SEM of about 3 points, so the 95% confidence interval is roughly ±6 points.
