To buy Canada’s average-priced home ($666,538, CREA, September 2026) with the minimum down payment, you need a household income of roughly $159,000 at today’s typical 4.94% 5-year fixed rate. That comes from the stress test: lenders qualify you at 6.94% and cap housing costs at 39% of gross income.

How lenders work it out

There is no fixed “income multiple” in Canada. Instead lenders compute the payment at the qualifying rate (6.94% for a 4.94% fixed), add property tax, heating and half of any condo fees, and require that total to be no more than 39% of gross income (GDS). All debts together must stay under 44% (TDS). As a rough guide at today’s rates, the maximum mortgage is about 4.7× gross income before property tax — less once tax and heating are included.

What lowers the income you need

  • A variable rate qualifies at 5.95% instead of 6.94%.
  • A 30-year amortization (first-time buyers, new builds, or 20% down).
  • A bigger down payment — smaller loan and no CMHC premium at 20%.
  • Clearing car loans and credit card balances before applying.
  • Lower property taxes — they vary widely between municipalities.

What it feels like on take-home pay

At $159,000 a single Ontario earner takes home about $9,130 a month in 2026, and the real payment on the average home ($3,758) is 41% of that before property tax, utilities and insurance. See the $160,000 salary after tax page for the full breakdown.

FAQ

How much income do I need to buy a house in Canada?

For the $666,538 national average home (CREA, September 2026) with the minimum down payment, about $159,000 of household income at a 4.94% fixed rate, assuming no other debts.

How much house can I afford on $100,000 a year in Canada?

With no other debts, a 5-year fixed at 4.94% and the stress test, roughly a mortgage of $392,000, assuming $5,000 of annual property tax.

What is the GDS ratio for a mortgage in Canada?

Gross debt service is housing costs (stress-tested mortgage payment, property tax, heating and half of condo fees) divided by gross income. CMHC allows up to 39%.

Does a variable rate let me borrow more?

Yes, because the qualifying rate is the contract rate + 2: 5.95% for a 3.95% variable versus 6.94% for a 4.94% fixed.

Assumptions & sources

  • Bank of Canada raised the target for the overnight rate by 25 basis points to 2.50% on 28 October 2026 (Bank Rate 2.75%, deposit rate 2.45%); next decision 9 December 2026. Big-bank prime rose to 4.70% on 29 October 2026.
  • Typical rates: 4.94% 5-year fixed and 3.95% 5-year variable are the median of the 17 lenders listed in Ratehub.ca’s rate tables updated 2 November 2026 (fixed range 4.44%–5.99%, variable 3.68%–4.70%). Insured (high-ratio) mortgages often get slightly lower rates than uninsured ones.
  • Fixed-rate payments use Canadian semi-annual compounding (Interest Act); variable-rate payments use monthly compounding, which most lenders apply to variable mortgages. Rates are assumed constant for the whole amortization.
  • CMHC homeowner premiums on the loan amount: 4.00% above 90% loan-to-value, 3.10% for 85.01–90%, 2.80% for 80.01–85%, plus 0.20% for amortizations over 25 years. Provincial sales tax on the premium in Ontario (8%), Quebec (9%) and Saskatchewan (6%) must be paid in cash at closing.
  • Minimum down payment (Department of Finance rules from 15 December 2024): 5% of the first $500,000, 10% of the portion from $500,000 to $1.5 million; 20% at $1.5 million or more, where default insurance is not available. 30-year insured amortizations: first-time buyers and buyers of new builds.
  • Stress test: qualify at the greater of 5.25% or the contract rate + 2 points (OSFI Guideline B-20; the same floor applies to insured loans). Income needed uses CMHC’s 39% GDS / 44% TDS limits, property tax of 1% of the price a year and $100/month heating. Take-home pay: 2026 Ontario, from our Canada salary after tax engine.
  • National average price: CREA, September 2026 ($666,538, −1.6% year over year).
