Since 15 December 2024, all first-time home buyers and all buyers of newly built homes in Canada can get a 30-year amortization on an insured mortgage (less than 20% down). Everyone else with under 20% down is limited to 25 years. The longer amortization lowers the payment by roughly 8–9% at today’s rates but adds a 0.20% CMHC surcharge and tens of thousands in interest.

Who can choose a 30-year amortization?

The rules were changed in two steps. From 1 August 2024, 30-year insured amortizations were opened to first-time buyers purchasing new builds. In September 2024 the federal government announced the expansion that took effect on 15 December 2024: any first-time buyer (new or resale home) and any buyer of a newly built home, first-time or not.

  • First-time home buyer — at least one borrower who has never bought a home, has not occupied a home owned by them or their current spouse or common-law partner in the last four years, or has recently experienced the breakdown of a marriage or common-law relationship.
  • New build — a home that has not been previously occupied for residential purposes.
  • Uninsured mortgages (20% or more down) — not covered by these rules at all; most lenders already offer up to 30 years.

What 30 years does to the payment

On a $700,000 home with the minimum $45,000 down, the 25-year mortgage is $681,200 (4.00% premium) and costs $3,939 a month at 4.94%. With 30 years the premium is 4.20%, the loan $682,510, and the payment $3,618 — $321 a month less. But over the full amortization you would pay $1,302,500 instead of $1,181,600: $120,900 more.

The real benefit: qualifying

For many buyers the payment saving matters less than the stress test. Because the qualifying payment is spread over 360 months instead of 300, the income needed for the same home falls by several thousand dollars — on the $700,000 example, about $9,000. That can be the difference between approval and rejection.

The trade-offs

Slower equity. After the first 5-year term the 30-year loan still owes $626,000, versus $602,000 on 25 years — $23,000 less equity if you need to sell.
Higher premium. The 0.20% surcharge on a $655,000 loan is $1,310, plus interest on it.
You can shorten it later. Take the 30-year amortization for approval and flexibility, then use prepayment privileges or increase the payment to the 25-year amount. Paying $3,939 on the 30-year loan would clear it in about 25 yrs 2 mo.

30 years vs accelerated payments

Choosing 30 years and then paying accelerated bi-weekly (half the 30-year payment, $1,809, every two weeks) would pay the loan off in about 25 yrs 4 mo — a middle path between flexibility and total cost.

FAQ

Can first-time buyers get a 30-year mortgage in Canada?

Yes. Since 15 December 2024 first-time home buyers can choose a 30-year amortization on an insured mortgage (less than 20% down), for both resale and newly built homes.

Who counts as a first-time home buyer for the 30-year amortization?

At least one borrower who has never purchased a home, has not lived in a home owned by them or their current spouse or common-law partner in the last four years, or has recently gone through the breakdown of a marriage or common-law partnership.

Is there an extra cost for a 30-year insured mortgage?

Yes. CMHC adds a 0.20% surcharge to the premium for amortizations over 25 years, for example 4.20% instead of 4.00% with less than 10% down.

How much lower is the payment with 30 years?

On a $700,000 home with the minimum down payment at 4.94%, about $321 a month: $3,618 instead of $3,939.

Can I get 30 years with 20% down?

Yes. Uninsured mortgages are not covered by the insured-mortgage rules, and most lenders offer amortizations of up to 30 years to anyone with at least 20% down.

  • CMHC homeowner premiums on the loan amount: 4.00% above 90% loan-to-value, 3.10% for 85.01–90%, 2.80% for 80.01–85%, plus 0.20% for amortizations over 25 years. Provincial sales tax on the premium in Ontario (8%), Quebec (9%) and Saskatchewan (6%) must be paid in cash at closing.
