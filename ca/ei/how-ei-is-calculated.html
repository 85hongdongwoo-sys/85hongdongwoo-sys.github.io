EI regular benefits are 55% of your average insurable weekly earnings, up to $729 a week in 2026. The average is not taken over the whole year: Service Canada adds up your earnings in your highest-paid “best weeks” — between 14 and 22 depending on unemployment in your region — and divides by that number of weeks. Here is the full formula with worked examples.

The EI formula in one line

Weekly benefit = (earnings in best weeks ÷ number of best weeks) × 55%, max $729.

The EI calculation step by step

  1. Find your qualifying period. Normally the 52 weeks before your claim starts, or the time since your last claim began if that is shorter. Only work in this window counts.
  2. Check you have enough insurable hours. Regular benefits need 420 to 700 hours depending on your region’s unemployment rate (the higher the rate, the fewer hours); sickness, maternity, parental and caregiving benefits need 600 hours everywhere.
  3. Look up your number of best weeks. It depends on the regional unemployment rate when your claim starts — 22 weeks where unemployment is 6% or less, falling one week per percentage point to 14 weeks at 13.1% or more.
  4. Add up insurable earnings in those weeks. Service Canada takes your highest-earning weeks from your Records of Employment, whether or not they are consecutive. Earnings count up to $68,900 a year (about $1,325 a week).
  5. Divide by the number of best weeks — even if you worked fewer weeks than that. This gives your average insurable weekly earnings.
  6. Multiply by 55% and round. The result can’t exceed $729. Low-income families with children may get the family supplement on top.
EI weekly = min($729, 55% × [earnings in best N weeks ÷ N])

The first example is the simplest: a steady salary makes every week identical. The second shows why the best-weeks rule matters for seasonal work: the weeks with no work and the weeks with fewer hours don’t drag the average down as long as you have at least 19 good weeks. The third is the trap — 15 weeks of earnings divided by 21 weeks cuts the average by almost a third. The fourth shows the ceiling: earnings above $1,325 a week are not insurable, so the benefit stops at $729.

What counts as insurable earnings?

Insurable earnings are generally the gross pay your employer reports on your Record of Employment: wages and salary, overtime, commissions, most bonuses, taxable benefits paid in cash, tips that are controlled by the employer, and vacation pay paid out during employment. They are the same earnings your EI premiums were deducted from — which is why they stop at $68,900, the same ceiling as the 1.63% premium. Severance and retiring allowances are not insurable earnings, so they never raise your benefit rate (and for claims starting by 9 October 2027 they don’t reduce it either).

What the formula does not include

  • Your salary on paper. Only what you actually earned in the qualifying period counts. A raise just before a layoff helps only if it shows up in your best weeks.
  • Your province. The rate and maximum are national; only the unemployment rate of your EI economic region matters (Quebec workers pay a lower premium because maternity and parental benefits come from QPIP).
  • Tax. The $729 maximum is before income tax, which Service Canada withholds from each payment — see is EI taxable.

FAQ

How is EI calculated in 2026?

Service Canada adds your insurable earnings in your best 14 to 22 weeks (depending on your region's unemployment rate), divides by that number of weeks and pays 55% of the result, up to $729 a week.

What are best weeks for EI?

They are your highest-paid weeks in the qualifying period (usually the last 52 weeks). The number used depends on the regional unemployment rate: 22 weeks at 6% or less, down to 14 weeks at 13.1% or more.

Does EI use gross or net pay?

Gross insurable earnings — pay before tax and deductions — up to $68,900 a year.

What if I worked fewer weeks than my best weeks number?

Your earnings are still divided by the full number of best weeks, so working fewer weeks lowers your average and your benefit.

Is overtime included in the EI calculation?

Yes. Overtime, commissions and most bonuses are insurable earnings and can increase your best weeks, up to the annual maximum.
